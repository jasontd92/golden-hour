# Coach profile: FIRST (Run Less, Run Faster) and running frequency (with Jeff Galloway)

## 1. Identity & credentials
- **FIRST** is the Furman Institute of Running and Scientific Training at Furman University, Greenville SC. Bill Pierce (chair of Health and Exercise Science, best marathon 2:44:50) and Scott Murr (PhD in exercise physiology, 2:46 marathoner, five-time Hawaii Ironman finisher) formed it in early 2003 with exercise physiologist Ray Moss, PhD, and Mickey McCauley (Burfoot, *Runner's World*, Aug 2004 [S1]).
- **Book:** *Run Less, Run Faster* by Pierce, Murr and Moss (Rodale, 2007; 2nd ed. 2012). The 3rd edition, by Pierce and Murr, came out Jan 2021 (Furman News, 2021 [S3]; furman.edu/first [S4]).
- **Jeff Galloway:** 1972 US Olympian (10,000 m) and creator of the run-walk-run method. Author of *Galloway's Book on Running* (Shelter Publications, Bolinas CA, 1984; 2nd ed. 2002). The 1984 edition's existence is confirmed by its Internet Archive record [S9]. The full text is access-restricted and **I could not read it.**

## 2. Domain in a hybrid program
How many runs per week, what each run is for, how hard each is paced, and what non-running aerobic work replaces the missing runs.

## 3. Core philosophy & mindset
- "Our most important objective is to help runners develop and maintain lifelong participation in running. Our second goal is to help them achieve as much as possible on a minimum of run training." (Pierce [S1])
- Origin: "Lo and behold, our 10-K, half-marathon, and marathon times didn't suffer at all… a three-day program, with some cross-training, was enough to maintain our running fitness." (Pierce [S1])
- Every run has a purpose: "With most runners, when I ask them what they're hoping to accomplish on a given run, they look back at me with a blank stare." (Pierce [S1])
- Murr: "We are guys with jobs and families who enjoy running, who want to be balanced, healthy and fit runners… We are not elite runners" [S5]. Murr also says the evidence "doesn't mean the FIRST training philosophy is better than other plans" [S5].
- Galloway: "The body maintains current improvements and is stimulated to improve best when running is done every other day." [S6]

## 4. Governing principles
1. **Three runs a week, and no more.** "This is the centerpiece of the entire FIRST program… This decreases the overall time commitment of the program, and the risk of injuries." [S1]
2. **Three distinct key runs.** A speed workout, a tempo run and a long run, "designed to improve your endurance, lactate-threshold running pace, and leg speed." [S1]
3. **Key runs are run faster than in conventional plans.** "The training paces recommended by the FIRST program are somewhat faster than those recommended by other training plans. Of course, with just three running days a week, you should be well rested for each workout." [S1]
4. **Hard cross-training twice a week.** "We believe that if you do cross-training correctly, you can use it to increase your overall training intensity, without increasing your injury risk… you can still go out and run hard the next day." The 2004 group "lollygagged" through cross-training, so FIRST began prescribing it explicitly. [S1]
5. **Cross-training is "a vital aspect"** and was later specified as two cycling workouts, one rowing workout and one swim workout per week (Murr [S5]). The 3rd edition lists swimming, rowing and stationary cycling (Furman News [S3]).
6. **Don't make up missed work.** "You can't make up what you missed… you certainly shouldn't double up on your workouts." Injury takes priority. [S1]
7. **Vary the speed work.** "Speedwork is much easier when you change it around a lot." [S1]
8. **Galloway: every other day, with true rest in between.** "If you want to sustain consistent improvement, you must commit to three running days a week… The day between should be a gentle exercise day to allow for recovery and re-building (no running)." [S6] "Having 48 hours between runs is like magic in repairing damage." "Those who insert a short and slow jog on recovery day (junk miles) are not allowing for complete recovery." [S7]

## 5. Concrete prescriptions & parameters
**Week layout (2004 marathon plan [S1]):** Tue speed, Thu tempo, Sat long run. Two other days of cross-training, "40 to 45 minutes."

**Paces, all relative to current 10K race pace in seconds per mile [S1]:**
- Long run: 10K + 60–75 s/mi ("60 to 75 seconds slower per mile than your 10-K race pace"; other plans are 30–40 s/mi slower still)
- Long tempo (8–10 mi): 10K + 30–35 s ≈ marathon goal pace
- Mid tempo (5–7 mi): 10K + 15–20 s
- Short tempo (3–4 mi): 10K pace
- 1600 m repeats: 10K − 35–40 s
- 1200 m repeats: 10K − 40–45 s
- 800 m repeats: 10K − 45–50 s
- 400 m repeats: 10K − 55–60 s
- Recovery between repeats: "generally taking just a 400-meter jog."

**Speed volume:** "Start modestly, but after a month, try to get the total distance of all the fast repeats to equal about three miles or 5000 meters (i.e., 5 × 1000 m, or 12 to 13 × 400 m)." [S1]

**Speed-session rotation (wk 1–15):** 8×400, 4×1200, 6×800, 3×1600, then increasing (10–12×400, 5–6×1200, 7–8×800, 3–4×1600). Tempo rotates 3/5/7/8/10 mi. [S1]

**Entry requirement:** able to run 10 miles (2004 study) [S1]. Murr: the marathon program "assumes that the runner is running at least 25 miles per week" [S5].

**Book pace tables:** later editions reportedly key paces off a recent 5K. I could not open the book to confirm the exact offsets, so for formulas use the 10K-based 2004 table above.

**Galloway:** three running days with a no-running gentle-exercise day between [S6]. After a race, "one rest/easy day for each mile of the race" (e.g., no speed for 6 days after a 10K) [SECONDARY, S8].

## 6. Progression, testing & measurement
- FIRST tests VO2max, lactate threshold, running economy and body composition, then sets individual paces from those results (Furman News [S2]).
- **2003 pilot** (pre/post lab tests, no restriction on extra running): running economy +2%, VO2max +4.8%, LT pace +4.4% [S1].
- **2004 Kiawah Island study** (16 wk, 25 selected, ≤3 runs/wk, 2 cross-training days encouraged): 21 ran the marathon and all finished. 15 set PRs, and 4 of the other 6 beat their most recent time. VO2max +4.2%, LT speed +2.3%, body fat −8.7%. Two injury-related step-downs to the half and one injury dropout. Pierce: "I had expected that we would lose at least five runners to injuries." [S1]
- **Limitations:** about 25 subjects per study, **no control group**, and self-selected volunteers. No peer-reviewed paper on the 2003–2005 studies was found. A Europe PMC and PubMed search turned up only a later Murr & Pierce paper on aging runners (2019) [S10]. Murr says "we conducted three training studies" [S5], but I found no journal publication of them.
- Progression: build over 13 weeks to two 20-mile runs, then a 3-week taper [S1].

## 7. Explicitly rejects / warns against
- "Junk miles" and runs with no stated purpose (Pierce [S1]; Galloway [S7]).
- Doubling up to make up missed workouts [S1].
- Easy cross-training: done "correctly" it should be hard [S1].
- Galloway: a short jog on the recovery day [S7]; ramping up too fast: "If a little running feels good, they'll run a lot more, get injured or burned out." [S6]

## 8. Does not address
- Strength training dose and programming beyond Murr's personal anecdote ("convinced that the strength training is helping me avoid injury") [S5].
- Concurrent heavy lifting, metcons, or how a CrossFit-style load counts against "hard cross-training."
- 10K-specific plans in the sources I could open (the 2004 article covers the marathon).
- Polarized/80-20 intensity distribution. FIRST's three key runs are all moderate-to-hard; it has no easy runs.

## 9. Stated tensions with other approaches
- FIRST says outright that it "defies the conventional wisdom" of high mileage. Its long runs are run faster than in "many other marathon programs" [S1].
- [INFERENCE] It conflicts with polarized/80-20 models (Seiler). All three FIRST runs sit at or above marathon pace, and its low-intensity volume comes from cross-training rather than easy running.
- [INFERENCE] With Galloway: FIRST's cross-training is "hard," while Galloway's day between runs is "gentle."

## Frequency evidence

**The 1970s source and Galloway's S-curve claim.**
- The "S-curve" claim is confirmed only secondhand, from a review of the 2002 2nd edition: "There is a dramatic increase in fitness if you run three – as opposed to one or two days per week… only small gains to be had by running four, five, six, or seven days per week" [SECONDARY, S8]. The reviewer adds, "Galloway doesn't get wrapped up in summarizing the latest scientific studies." **The cited study is not confirmed.**
- The most likely source [INFERENCE] is Michael Pollock's group, the only 1970s randomized work comparing 1, 3 and 5 days/week:
  - **Gettman, Pollock et al. 1976**, *Res Q* 47(4):638–646, "Physiological responses of men to 1, 3, and 5 day per week training programs" (PMID 1070734). Results not obtained.
  - **Pollock et al. 1977**, *Med Sci Sports* 9(1):31–36 (PMID 870782) [S11]. 20-week jogging program at 85–90% HRmax, 30 min, 1, 3 or 5 days/week. "Cardiorespiratory fitness improved in direct proportion to frequency and duration of training." Injury rates were **0%, 12% and 39%** for 1, 3 and 5 days/week. Injury attrition was 0%, 4% and 6%. The 5-day program was "not recommended for beginning joggers because of the significantly greater percent of injuries."
  - So Pollock found **linear fitness gains, not a plateau**. What rose steeply past 3 days was injury. The "sweet spot" is a fitness-per-injury trade-off in untrained beginners, not diminishing fitness returns.
- **Wenger & Bell 1986**, *Sports Med* 3(5):346–356 (PMID 3529283) [S12]. Frequency as low as 2/week improves less-fit people, "but when aerobic power exceeds 50 ml/kg/min, exercise frequency of at least 3 times per week is required." Pooled data suggest maximal gains at "intensities between 90 to 100% VO2max, 4 times per week with exercise durations of 35 to 45 minutes." Gains shrink as starting fitness rises.
- I did not look into Kenneth Cooper's *Aerobics* (1968) or link it to Galloway.

**Current evidence (2012+).**
- **Frequency vs VO2max:**
  - Yang et al. 2025, network meta-analysis of interval training in athletes (*BMC Sports Sci Med Rehabil* 17:156) [S13]. For HIIT, 3 sessions/week "yielded superior outcomes compared to 2 sessions per week" (g = 1.21 vs 0.69). More than 3 sessions/week was not significant (g = 1.02, p = 0.15). The authors warn that the subgroups are thin.
  - Lenk et al. 2025 (*Physiol Rep* 13:e70573) [S14]: 4×4-min HIIT 1×, 2× or 3×/week for 6 weeks, n = 26, VO2max about 51. 1×/week did nothing. 2× and 3× improved, "with no clear additional benefit from increasing the frequency to three sessions per week." Exploratory.
  - Together: for hard sessions, 2–3/week is the effective range and there is no evidence that more helps. This supports FIRST's design of three quality runs.
- **Volume vs performance:** Tanda 2011 (*J Hum Sport Exerc* 6(3):511–520) [S15]. Marathon time in recreational runners was predicted mainly by mean weekly distance and mean training pace. Volume matters, and frequency is one way to raise it.
- **Cross-training transfer:** Menges et al. 2026 meta-analysis of running vs cycling (*Front Sports Act Living* 8:1843803) [S16]. It "found no statistically significant differences in VO2max or running performance." However, the evidence "does not support conclusions regarding the interchangeability of training modalities." The trials are small and old. This partly supports FIRST's premise that cross-training can carry aerobic load, but it does not prove equivalence.
- **Injury:**
  - Nielsen et al. 2012 systematic review (*Int J Sports Phys Ther*, PMID 22389869) [S17]: "Conflicting results were reported on the relationships between volume, duration, intensity, and frequency and RRI."
  - Frandsen et al. 2025, 5,205 runners, 588,071 sessions (*BJSM* 59:1203–1210, doi:10.1136/bjsports-2024-109380) [S18]. Overuse injury rose when a **single session exceeded the longest run of the prior 30 days by >10%**: hazard ratio 1.64 for 10–30% over, 2.28 for >100% over. The week-to-week load ratio showed no association.
- **Net reading:**
  - Three runs/week is a well-supported **minimum effective dose** for quality work. It is not a proven optimum for a trained runner chasing PRs.
  - Newer evidence does not show a fitness plateau after three runs. Pollock's gains were linear, and Wenger & Bell's pooled optimum was four sessions/week.
  - The injury case against more frequency comes from untrained beginners in the 1970s, and modern reviews call the frequency–injury link conflicting.
  - [INFERENCE] With only three runs, the only way to add volume is longer single sessions, which is the pattern Frandsen links to injury.
