# Coach profile: Stephen Seiler, endurance training intensity distribution (80/20, polarized)

## 1. Identity & credentials
- Stephen Seiler, PhD, is an American-born exercise physiologist and professor at the University of Agder (Kristiansand, Norway). His research on how successful endurance athletes distribute training intensity is the origin of the "80:20 rule" and the "polarized" training model. (Seiler & Tønnessen 2009; Athletics Weekly 2016.)
- His key papers include Seiler & Kjerland 2006 (Scand J Med Sci Sports), Seiler & Tønnessen 2009 (Sportscience), Seiler 2010 (IJSPP) and Seiler et al. 2013 (Scand J Med Sci Sports, 4×4/4×8/4×16). He co-authored Foster et al. 2022 (MSSE) and the 2025 individual-participant network meta-analysis (Rosenblat … Seiler, Sports Med).

## 2. Domain in a hybrid program
How endurance work, especially running, is distributed across intensity zones: how much is easy, how much is hard, how hard sessions are built, and how many there are each week.

## 3. Core philosophy & mindset
- The elite pattern is evidence of evolution, not a theory: "any consistent pattern of training intensity distribution emerging across sport disciplines is likely to be a result of a successful self-organization (evolution) towards a 'population optimum.'" (Seiler & Tønnessen 2009)
- A population optimum is not a personal rule: "High performance training is an individualized process for sure, but by population optimum, we mean an approach to training organization that results in most athletes staying healthy, making good progress, and performing well in their most important events." (ibid.)
- Easy days and hard days have different jobs: "Some days you build the cake (train long and steady, for example), other days you eat the cake." "Some sessions should be psychologically relaxing to perform. Others should test you." (Athletics Weekly 2016, interview)
- "The worst thing to do is let the easy sessions become harder in search of 'a little extra'." (ibid.)
- Easy volume is purposeful: "These sessions are developmental, not trash miles." (ibid.)

## 4. Governing principles
1. **Count by session.** "About 80 % of training sessions are performed completely or predominantly at intensities under the first ventilatory turn point, or a blood-lactate concentration ≤2mM. The remaining ~20 % of sessions are distributed between training at or near the traditional lactate threshold (Zone 2), and training at intensities in the 90-100 %VO2max range, generally as interval training (Zone 3)." (Seiler & Tønnessen 2009)
2. **Session goal, not heart-rate time-in-zone.** In junior skiers, time-in-zone gave 91/6/2.6% (Z1/Z2/Z3), while session-goal counting gave about 75/5/17–20%. "Typical software-based heart-rate analysis methods overestimate the amount of time spent training at low intensity … the unit of stress perceived and responded to by the athlete is the stress of the entire training session or perhaps training day, not minutes in any given heart-rate zone." (Seiler & Tønnessen 2009; Seiler & Kjerland 2006)
3. **Polarized distribution.** Seiler & Kjerland (2006) proposed that the optimum "approximated a 'polarized distribution' with 75-80 % of training sessions in Zone 1, 5 % in Zone 2, and 15-20 % in Zone 3. However, there is considerable variation in how athletes competing in different sports and event durations distribute their training intensity within Zones 2 and 3." (quoted in Seiler & Tønnessen 2009)
4. **A small number of hard sessions.** "An elite athlete training 10-12 times per week is therefore likely to dedicate 1-3 sessions weekly to training at intensities at or above the maximum lactate steady state. This rule of thumb coincides well with training studies demonstrating the efficacy of adding two interval sessions per week." (Seiler & Tønnessen 2009)
5. **More HIT is not better.** In Billat et al. (1999), going from 1 HIT + 1 LT session to 3 HIT + 1 LT "gave no additional adaptive benefit, but did increase subjective training stress and indicators of impending overtraining." Also: "training intensification over periods of 2-8 wk with frequent high-intensity bouts (3-4 sessions per week) is an effective means of temporarily compromising performance and inducing overreaching." (Seiler & Tønnessen 2009)
6. **Accumulated work at slightly lower intensity beats shorter, harder work.** "Accumulating 32 min of work at 90% HR max induces greater adaptive gains than accumulating 16 min of work at ∼95% HR max despite lower RPE." (Seiler et al. 2013) "A tiny decrease in intensity translates to a large increase in accumulated work time." (Athletics Weekly 2016)
7. **Avoid the threshold "black hole".** "It is hard to keep recreational people training 45-60 min a day 3-5 days a week from accumulating a lot of training time at their lactate threshold. Training intended to be longer and slower becomes too fast and shorter in duration, and interval training fails to reach the desired intensity." (Seiler & Tønnessen 2009)
8. **Specificity.** In the interview Seiler is reported as saying the best way to train for running is running [paraphrase; Athletics Weekly 2016].

## 5. Concrete prescriptions & parameters
**Three-zone model (Seiler & Tønnessen 2009, Fig. 1):** thresholds are set by ventilatory turnpoints VT1 and VT2, which roughly match lactate ~2 mM and ~4 mM / MLSS.
- Z1: below VT1 / ≤2 mM.
- Z2: between VT1 and VT2 (around "traditional lactate threshold").
- Z3: above VT2, ~90–100% VO2max, usually intervals.
- Five-zone Norwegian scale (Table 1), %HRmax / lactate:
  - Z1: 55–75% / 0.8–1.5
  - Z2: 75–85% / 1.5–2.5
  - Z3: 85–90% / 2.5–4
  - Z4: 90–95% / 4–6
  - Z5: 95–100% / 6–10
  - Five-zone Z3 ≈ three-zone Z2. The source warns this scale "does not account for individual variation in the relationship between heart rate and blood lactate."
- Zones 6–8 are "anaerobic": sprint, anaerobic capacity and strength.
- RPE: session RPE (Foster method) agreed 92% with session-goal HR classification (Seiler & Tønnessen 2009).

**Interval designs:**
- 4×8 min at maximal sustainable effort with 2 min recovery, 2×/week plus 2–3 low-intensity sessions. This ran at ~90% HRpeak and ~9.6 mM lactate. It gave the largest gains in VO2peak, power at VO2peak and power at 4 mM (+11.4% vs 4.2–5.6% for the other groups; P<0.02). (Seiler et al. 2013, trained recreational cyclists, ~6 h/wk, 7 wk)
- With self-selected pace, 4-min work / 2-min recovery gave the highest physiological response with maintained speed. However, "perceptual and physiological response differences across the typical work and recovery spectrum are fairly small … a variety of combinations of work and rest duration are effective." (Seiler & Tønnessen 2009)
- Elite sessions from Table 6 (Aasen 2008). Five-zone Z3 (threshold): 6×15 min, 5×10 min, 8×8 min with 2-min recovery, or LT 40–60 min. Five-zone Z4: 10×6, 8×5, 15×3, 40×1 min. "For those without the same training base, similar workouts would be performed but with less total effective duration."
- Pace intervals so the first rep's output can be held for all reps [SECONDARY: Fast Talk Labs description of Seiler's 4×8 advice].

## 6. Progression, testing & measurement
- Quantify intensity per session: classify each session by goal and check it against session RPE or HR. Raw HR time-in-zone undercounts hard work (Seiler & Kjerland 2006).
- Set zones from a lab test (VT1/VT2 or lactate). %HRmax tables are approximations (Seiler & Tønnessen 2009).
- Development comes mostly from adding frequency and volume over the years. Younger athletes train 5–8 times/week and mature elites 10–13; intensification of already well-trained athletes has not shown convincing long-term gains (Seiler 2010 abstract, as reported) [SECONDARY for exact wording].
- Seiler does not give a weekly volume-progression percentage in the sources reviewed.

## 7. Explicitly rejects / warns against
- Letting easy sessions drift harder, or hard sessions drift easier (black hole). Foster et al. (2001) "found that athletes tend to run harder on easy days and easier on hard days" (cited in Seiler & Tønnessen 2009).
- 3–4 HIT sessions/week for weeks at a time (overreaching).
- Equating maximum intensity with maximum benefit (Seiler et al. 2013).
- Hype and gadgetry over the traditional process [paraphrase; Athletics Weekly 2016].
- The Pareto reading that elites "waste" their 80% easy training (Seiler & Tønnessen 2009, "Intensified-Training Studies").

## 8. Does not address (boundaries)
- Barbell programming, metcons/CrossFit, gymnastics, body composition: not addressed in the sources reviewed.
- Strength training: elite rowers and speed skaters "accumulate substantial additional hours of strength training", and Olaf Tufte's training was "about 92 % … endurance training, with the remainder being primarily strength training" (Seiler & Tønnessen 2009). The sources record no rule for how strength sessions count against the 80/20. A Pez Cycling piece reports Seiler saying strength benefits for endurance athletes are individual [SECONDARY].
- Whether non-running high-intensity work (metcons, assault bike) counts as "hard sessions" in the running distribution: not addressed. [INFERENCE] By his session-goal and whole-day stress logic ("the stress of the entire training session or perhaps training day"), a hard metcon is a hard stress day.
- A specific number of runs per week for recreational runners: not prescribed. He describes recreational athletes as training "4-5 times and 6-10 hours per week".

## 9. Stated tensions with other approaches
- **Threshold-heavy programs** (ACSM-style, between-thresholds work): Esteve-Lanao's polarized group improved 10 km more than an ACSM-style group (Seiler & Tønnessen 2009). Muñoz et al. 2014 found no significant full-sample 10 km difference (5.0% vs 3.6%). Only a post hoc subset of 12 compliant runners favored polarized.
- **HIIT-dominant programs:** see principle 5. Stöggl & Sperlich 2014 (not Seiler-authored; cited in this literature) found polarized training gave the largest gains (VO2peak +11.7%) over threshold, HIIT and high-volume groups in 48 trained athletes over 9 weeks.
- **Pyramidal vs polarized debate:** Foster, Casado, Esteve-Lanao, Haugen & Seiler (MSSE 2022) argued "polarized is optimal". Burnley, Bearden & Jones replied that pyramidal (Z1>Z2>Z3) is what athletes actually do. In that exchange the Seiler side called pyramidal a "near cousin" that may be as effective [SECONDARY summary].
- **Elite-to-amateur transfer:** "Is the pattern of 80 % below and 20 % above lactate threshold appropriate for recreational athletes training 4-5 times and 6-10 hours per week? There are almost no published data addressing this question." (Seiler & Tønnessen 2009) Interview: "Although we cannot train as often as the elites, this basic pattern still works!" (Athletics Weekly 2016). A podcast host reports Seiler agreeing that 80/20 describes 25–30 h weeks and does not carry over directly to ~6 h weeks [SECONDARY, paraphrase].

## Current state of evidence (brief, honest)
- **Oliveira, Boppre & Fonseca 2024** (Sports Med; 17 studies, n=437; cites Seiler extensively). "POL is superior to other TIDs for improving VO2peak, particularly in shorter duration interventions" (SMD 0.24). The advantage appeared only in <12-week interventions and highly trained athletes. "The effect of POL was similar to that of other TIDs on the remaining surrogates of endurance performance": time trial SMD −0.01, plus time-to-exhaustion and threshold speed. "POL more effectively improves aerobic power but is similar to other TIDs for improving aerobic capacity."
- **Rosenblat … Seiler 2025** (Sports Med, IPD network meta-analysis; 13 studies, n=348, 150 recreational). There was "no difference in VO2max (SMD = −0.06) or TT performance (SMD = −0.05) between POL and pyramidal". "Athletes at a more competitive level may benefit from a POL TID intervention and recreational athletes from a PYR TID intervention."
- **Bottom line:** the robust finding is the low-intensity majority, about 75–80% of sessions easy. Polarized vs pyramidal is roughly equivalent for performance. For recreational athletes, the paper Seiler co-authored leans pyramidal (some Z2/threshold work). The 4×8 result comes from one 7-week study of 35 cyclists.
