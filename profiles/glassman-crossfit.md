# Coach Profile: Greg Glassman / CrossFit (original methodology)

*Every source below was opened and read in full: original CrossFit Journal (CFJ) PDF reprints from library.crossfit.com, plus the CrossFit Level 1 Training Guide (3rd ed., © 2020). The L1 Guide notes that "some edits to the original articles have been made." Where its wording differs from the original article, that is flagged.*

## 1. Identity & credentials
- Greg Glassman founded CrossFit, Inc. and CrossFit Santa Cruz with Lauren Glassman, and published the CrossFit Journal (author bio in "A Theoretical Template…", CFJ Issue 06, Feb 2003).
- The L1 Training Guide calls itself "a collection of CrossFit Journal articles written since 2002 primarily by CrossFit Founder Coach Greg Glassman" (L1 Guide, p. i).
- He claims legitimacy from results, not theory: "we are not attempting to demonstrate our program's legitimacy through scientific principles. We are but sharing the methods of a program whose legitimacy has been established through the testimony of athletes, soldiers, cops, and others" ("What Is Fitness?", CFJ Oct 2002).

## 2. Domain in a hybrid program
General physical preparedness (GPP): broad, general, inclusive work capacity across time and modal domains, built with constantly varied, high-intensity functional movement.

## 3. Core philosophy & mindset
- **Prescription:** "CrossFit is: 'constantly varied, high-intensity functional movement.'" ("Understanding CrossFit," Apr 2007, L1 Guide p. 2)
- **Generalism:** "Our specialty is not specializing. Combat, survival, many sports, and life reward this kind of fitness and, on average, punish the specialist." ("What Is Fitness?", Oct 2002)
- **Preparing for the unknown:** the aim is to "prepare them not only for the unknown but for the unknowable." ("Understanding CrossFit," 2007)
- **Against routine:** "Routine is the enemy." ("World-Class Fitness in 100 Words," in "What Is Fitness?", 2002). Also: "There is no ideal routine! In fact, the chief value of any routine lies in abandoning it for another." ("Foundations," Apr 2002)
- **Empiricism:** "The methodology that drives CrossFit is entirely empirical… measurable, observable, repeatable data. We call this approach 'evidence-based fitness.'" ("Understanding CrossFit," 2007)
- **Work capacity above everything else:** "We have come to see increased work capacity as the Holy Grail of performance improvement… We would not trade improvements in any other fitness metric for a decrease in work capacity." (ibid.)
- **Fundamentals:** "Virtuosity is defined in gymnastics as 'performing the common uncommonly well.'" ("Fundamentals, Virtuosity and Mastery," Aug 2005, L1 Guide p. 160)

## 4. Governing principles
1. **Definition of fitness.** "CrossFit increases work capacity across broad time and modal domains." Fitness is measured as area under a power-vs-duration curve. ("Understanding CrossFit," 2007; "What Is Fitness? (Part 2)," adapted from the 21 Feb 2009 L1 lecture, L1 Guide pp. 32–39)
2. **First standard: 10 general physical skills.** These are cardiovascular/respiratory endurance, stamina, strength, flexibility, power, speed, coordination, agility, balance and accuracy. "You are as fit as you are competent in each of these ten skills." Endurance, stamina, strength and flexibility come through *training* ("measurable organic change"). Coordination, agility, balance and accuracy come through *practice* ("changes in the nervous system"). Power and speed come from both. ("What Is Fitness?", 2002) *Note: the 2002 body text omits speed from its list even though it says "ten"; the sidebar lists all ten.*
3. **Second standard: the hopper.** Picture a hopper of countless physical tasks drawn at random: "your fitness can be measured by your capacity to perform well at these tasks in relation to other individuals." It "encourages the athlete to disinvest in any set notions of sets, rest periods, reps, exercises, order of exercises, routines, periodization." (2002) The 2009 lecture adds: "There is more traction… in pursuing headlong that event or skill that you do not want to see come out of the hopper." (L1 Guide p. 33)
4. **Third standard: three metabolic pathways.**
   - Phosphagen: "less than about ten seconds."
   - Glycolytic: "up to several minutes."
   - Oxidative: "in excess of several minutes."
   - "Total fitness… requires competency and training in each." (2002)
   - The 2009 lecture gives these as about 100% / ~70% / ~40% of max power output. The glycolytic pathway "peaks at about 60 seconds and terminates at 120 seconds." (L1 Guide p. 34)
5. **Sickness, wellness, fitness.** These lie on one continuum. "Fitness is and should be 'super-wellness.'… A fitness regimen that doesn't support health is not CrossFit." (2002) In 2009, health is defined as fitness held across age, with age as a Z-axis (L1 Guide p. 38).
6. **Theoretical hierarchy of development.** "It starts with nutrition and moves to metabolic conditioning, gymnastics, weightlifting, and finally sport… We don't deliberately order these components but nature will. If you have a deficiency at any level of 'the pyramid' the components above will suffer." (2002) "Foundations" lists metabolic conditioning as "beginning with aerobic, then lactic acid, and then phosphocreatine pathways."
7. **Breadth of adaptation matches breadth of stimulus.** "Any routine, no matter how complete, contains within its omissions the parameters for which there will be no adaptation." ("Foundations," 2002) And: "Your body will only respond to an unaccustomed stressor." ("What Is Fitness?", 2002)
8. **Functional movement.** Functional movements are "universal motor recruitment patterns… performed in a wave of contraction from core to extremity… compound." ("Understanding CrossFit," 2007) Neuroendocrine response, power, cross-training and functional movement are "the four defining themes." ("Foundations," 2002)
9. **Intensity is power.** "Intensity is defined exactly as power," and it is "the independent variable most commonly associated with maximizing the rate of return of favorable adaptation." (2007)
10. **Mechanics, then consistency, then intensity.** "CrossFit's charter for creating the most optimal balance of safety, efficacy, and efficiency is: mechanics, consistency, then—and only then—intensity." ("Scaling CrossFit," L1 Guide p. 77) Glassman, quoted in the "Technique" article: "'Mechanics,' then 'Consistency,' and then 'Intensity'—this is the key to effective implementation of CrossFit programming." (L1 Guide p. 40)
11. **Blur cardio and strength.** "Strive to blur distinctions between 'cardio' and strength training. Nature has no regard for this distinction." (2002)
12. **Scale by degree, not kind.** "The needs of an Olympic athlete and our grandparents differ by degree not kind… We scale load and intensity; we don't change programs." (2002)
13. **Programming comes from practice, not science.** "No successful strength and conditioning program has anywhere ever been derived from scientific principles… Programming derives from clinical practice and can only be justified or legitimized by the results of that practice." (Glassman quote, L1 Guide p. 75; no original date given)

## 5. Concrete prescriptions & parameters
**Weekly frequency and session length**
- "Five or six days per week mix these elements." ("100 Words," 2002)
- "Three days on and one day off allows for a maximum sustainability at maximum intensities." (2002)
- "An hour a day six days per week… intensity… is not sustainable past forty-five minutes to an hour… Past one hour, more is not better!" ("Foundations," 2002)

**Theoretical Template** (CFJ Issue 06, Feb 2003)
- Modalities are M (monostructural metabolic conditioning), G (gymnastics) and W (weightlifting).
- 12-day macro cycle, with "M = one day, MG = two" etc. shown in full:
  - Days 1–4: M | G-W | M-G-W | OFF
  - Days 5–8: G | W-M | G-W-M | OFF
  - Days 9–12: W | M-G | W-M-G | OFF
- The alternative is 5 on / 2 off. "If life is easier with the five-on, two-off pattern, do not hesitate to employ it."
- **Single-element days (1, 5, 9)** are "element priority." M is a "long, slow distance" single effort. G is a "single skill" practised for a long time. W is a "single lift… high weight and low repetition." "Recovery is not a limiting factor… rest is long and deliberate."
- **Two-element days (2, 6, 10)** are "task priority… the task is set and the time varies." A couplet is done for "3-5 rounds performed for time" with "moderate to high intensity," and "work-rest interval management is critical." "Ideally, the first round is hard but possible."
- **Three-element days (3, 7, 11)** are "time priority." A triplet runs "for a specified number of minutes (typically 20)" and is scored by rounds. Elements are "lightly to moderately challenging," and work/rest is "a marginal factor."
- "Each M, W, and G represents a single exercise."
- How the template relates to variance: the template is "as much descriptive as prescriptive," "randomized within some parameters," and "each three-day cycle is a singularly unique stimulus." The original says the template "did not generate our Workout of the Day."
  - *The L1 Guide edit adds: "CrossFit.com has never used this template for its programming, but it provides new trainers a way to effectively apply variance."*
- Exercises by modality (Table 2):
  - M: run, bike, row, jump rope.
  - G: air squat, pull-up, push-up, dip, handstand push-up (HSPU), rope climb, muscle-up, press to handstand, back extension, sit-up, jump, lunge.
  - W: deadlift, cleans, press, snatch, clean and jerk, medicine-ball drills, kettlebell swing.

**Other session patterns** ("What Is Fitness?", 2002)
- Lift, then gymnastics, then metcon: warm up; "three to five sets of three to five reps of a fundamental lift at a moderately comfortable pace"; then a 10-minute gymnastics circuit "at a blistering pace"; then "two to ten minutes of high intensity metabolic conditioning." "There is nothing sacred in this pattern."
- Couplet: e.g., 5 moderately heavy back squats plus max pull-ups, 3–5 times.
- Circuit: 5–6 balanced elements, done three times without a break.

**Heavy days**
- The single-W day (above).
- Example heavy-day programming: "Deadlift 5-3-3-2-2-2-1-1-1." (Template, 2003)
- From the L1 lesson plan:
  - Back squat 5-5-5-5-5, "single-modality weightlifting heavy day."
  - Rest "3-5 minutes between working sets."
  - "New personal records should be attempted in the third or fourth set." (L1 Guide p. 92)

**Metabolic conditioning intervals** ("What Is Fitness?", 2002)

| Pathway | Work | Rest | Ratio | Repeats |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Phosphagen | 10–30 s | 30–90 s | 1:3 | 25–30 |
| Glycolytic | 30–120 s | 60–240 s | 1:2 | 10–20 |
| Oxidative | 120–300 s | 120–300 s | 1:1 | not stated |

- "The bulk of metabolic training should be interval training."
- Tabata: "20 seconds of work followed by 10 seconds of rest repeated six to eight times."
- "It is highly desirable to regularly experiment with interval patterns."
- Distance mix: "For every long distance effort our athletes will do five or six at short distance." ("Foundations," 2002)
- Aim: maximise "first wave" (VO2max) adaptations and get the second wave (lactate threshold) "systemically through multiple modalities." The third wave (efficiency) is to be avoided "completely." (2002, interpreting Seiler)

**Gymnastics benchmarks** (2002)
- Pull-ups: 20 / 25 / 30.
- Push-ups: 50 / 75 / 100.
- Dips: 20 / 30 / 40 / 50.
- Rope climbs without legs: 1–5.
- "At fifteen pull-ups and dips each it is time to start working regularly on a 'muscle-up.'"
- L-hold: up to 3 minutes, in 30-second steps.
- Handstand walk: 100 yards.
- "Every workout should contain regular gymnastic/calisthenic movements that you've mastered and other elements under development."

**Lifting**
- "Start your weightlifting career with the deadlift, clean, squat, and jerk then introduce the 'clean and jerk' and snatch." (2002)
- Session layout: "warm up, practice a basic movement or skill or pursue a new personal record (PR) or max lift… then pound out a tight little couplet or triplet." Play is "seasoning… not main courses." ("Virtuosity," 2005)

**Scaling** ("Scaling CrossFit," L1 Guide pp. 77–82)
- "Preserve the stimulus," meaning the combination of "movements, time domain, and load."
- Beginners reduce "intensity and/or volume by half for at least two weeks." Significant scaling should last at least a month.
- Order of adjustment:
  1. Load first. Choose a load that lets the athlete finish the first round "without compromising form or reaching muscular failure." New athletes often use "less than 50 percent of the prescribed load."
  2. Volume (time, reps or rounds, distance).
  3. Substitute the movement "last."
- "Err on the side of scaling too much." Fran "should be completed within several minutes."

**Nutrition (brief)**
- "Eat meat and vegetables, nuts and seeds, some fruit, little starch and no sugar. Keep intake to levels that will support exercise but not body fat." (2002)
- Zone: about 40% carbohydrate / 30% protein / 30% fat. "Dr. Barry Sears' Zone Diet… still offers the greatest precision." (2002)
- Protein of "0.7 and 1.0 grams… per pound of lean body mass." ("Foundations," 2002)
- Hyperinsulinism is framed as the root of disease. (Nutrition lecture, Sept/Oct 2007, L1 Guide p. 45)

## 6. Progression, testing & measurement
- **Benchmarks.** "Only by repeating workouts can we confidently measure our progress." The Girls (Angie, Barbara, Chelsea, Diane, Elizabeth, Fran) make "repeated, irregular, appearances" in the WOD. ("Benchmark Workouts," CFJ Issue 13, Sept 2003)
- **No special prep for tests.** "If you are doing the WOD, you are training for these workouts… if you get better at these you are becoming fitter." (2003)
- **Benchmarks expose weaknesses.** They are designed to "root out weaknesses." Each one is analysed for its metabolic character:
  - Barbara is a ~3-minute, 1:1 "aerobic interval."
  - Chelsea is 40 s on / 20 s off, a "hybrid interval like our Tabata." (2003)
- **Time measures power.** For a fixed-work benchmark, the ratio of times (T2/T1) gives the change in power, because the work cancels out. The "collection of workout data points represented your work capacity across broad time and modal domains." ("What Is Fitness? (Part 2)," 2009)
- **Scoring as sport.** "Using whiteboards as scoreboards, keeping accurate scores and records, running a clock… derive both relative and absolute metrics at every workout." ("Understanding CrossFit," 2007)
- **Comparing athletes.** Total capacity can be assessed by overall placing across "25 to 30 workouts" spanning row sprints to a 10-mile run. ("Technique," 1 Dec 2007 lecture)
- **Health check.** If any health marker moves "in a wrong direction… entertain the possibility you are doing something profoundly wrong." (2009)
- **Skill progressions.** The numeric gymnastics ladders in §5 serve as skill tests.

## 7. Explicitly rejects / warns against
- **Routine, fixed set/rep schemes and periodization dogma.** "Don't subscribe to high reps, or low reps, or long rests, or short rests, but strive for variance." (2002)
- **Isolation and bodybuilding moves.** "Curls, lateral raises, leg extensions, leg curls, flyes… have no place in a serious strength and conditioning program." (2002) "They have machines; we detest them." (2007 nutrition lecture)
- **Excessive aerobic volume.** "Favoring one or two [pathways] to the exclusion of the others and not recognizing the impact of excessive training in the oxidative pathway are arguably the two most common faults." (2002)
- **Sessions over an hour** ("Foundations").
- **Sport-specific S&C.** "The bulk of sport specific training has been ridiculously ineffective." (2002)
- **High-carb, low-fat diets** (2002).
- **The "novice's curse."** "Teaching a snatch where there is not yet an overhead squat… is a colossal mistake." Also, "rarely now do we see prescribed the short, intense couplets or triplets that epitomize CrossFit programming." ("Virtuosity," 2005)
- **Adding load before mechanics are sound.** Doing so "increases the risk for injury and potentially blunts long-term progress." ("Scaling CrossFit")
- **Advancing isolated lab metrics.** "Who would take an increase in VO2 max for a decrease in work capacity?" (2009)

## 8. Does not address (boundaries)
- [INFERENCE] There are no percentage-based load progressions, no RPE, no heart-rate zones and no planned deloads beyond 3-on/1-off. The sources reviewed give no numbers for these.
- [INFERENCE] There is no peaking or tapering method for a dated competition. The 2002 article rejects "set notions of… periodization."
- **Sport-specific needs:** "nearly completely met by regular practice and training within the sport" (2002). In other words, these are handed off to the sport rather than programmed.
- **The original sources reviewed do not specify:**
  - set volumes for accessory or hypertrophy work;
  - interval rep counts for the oxidative pathway;
  - how to set up a strength-bias cycle.
- **Not verified:**
  - formal "strength bias" programs (e.g., CFJ pieces by other authors), so they are not included;
  - CrossFit's later (post-2008) nutrition revisions beyond the L1 lectures above.

## 9. Stated tensions with other approaches
- **Endurance specialists.** "CrossFit considers the Sumo Wrestler, triathlete, marathoner, and power lifter to be 'fringe athletes'… Endurance athletes do not balance that compromise." ("Foundations," 2002) Outside Magazine calling a triathlete "fittest man on earth" is rejected (2002).
- **Mainstream exercise science.** The NSCA "doesn't even attempt a definition" of fitness. The ACSM's definition "contains nothing that can be measured." Mel Siff is criticised as having "fallen victim to junk science" over a high-carb diet. Exercise science "would very rarely meet the rigors of any real science." (2002; 2009)
- **Commercial gyms.** Glassman contrasts their "isolation movements and extended aerobic sessions" with CrossFit's "compound movements and shorter high intensity cardiovascular sessions." ("Foundations," 2002)
- **Mastery of a single modality.** CrossFit deliberately avoids "the efficiency that accompanies mastery of a single modality." It interprets Seiler's endurance research against Seiler's own intent ("it was not his intention"). (2002)
- [INFERENCE] There is a direct conflict with block or linear periodization and with high-volume aerobic base-building models. It follows from the statements above rather than from any named critique of a specific system.
